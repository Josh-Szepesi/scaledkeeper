Crested geckos are among the most approachable reptiles to breed in captivity. They tolerate room-temperature incubation, lay small clutches that are easy to manage, and raise hatchlings that respond well to straightforward setups. That accessibility can lead keepers to underestimate the preparation that actually makes a breeding season safe for the female and productive over time.

The gap between "my geckos mated" and "I ran a healthy breeding season" comes down to conditioning, monitoring, and catching problems before they compound. Underprepared females drain calcium reserves across a long laying season, lose body weight between clutches, and face real risk of reproductive complications. Poorly monitored eggs fail unnoticed. Hatchlings placed in oversized, high-humidity enclosures frequently refuse food for weeks.

This guide covers the full crested gecko breeding cycle: pre-breeding conditioning, courtship and pairing management, egg collection, incubation parameters, and hatchling setup. Each section includes the specific numbers - female weight minimums, incubation temperature ranges, laying box depth, and hatchling feeding timelines - along with the reasoning so you can adapt to your own situation. ## Pre-Breeding Conditioning: Getting Your Crested Geckos Ready

Weight minimums for breeding crested geckos are not arbitrary. A female converts significant calcium and protein into eggs across a season that can run six to eight months, and she needs meaningful reserves to draw on before that process begins. The commonly cited minimum female weight is 35 grams, but 40 grams is a more defensible starting point. A female entering her first breeding season at 33 grams will often fail to maintain weight through the second or third clutch, and that compounding drain is where metabolic bone disease and egg-binding risk become real concerns.

Males mature faster than females. Most males are reproductively capable at nine to twelve months and 25 grams. There is no harm in waiting until twelve months before pairing - a male in good condition at twelve months produces more reliably than a nine-month-old at minimum weight.

Before pairing, assess both animals honestly using a body condition score. A female ready for breeding shows no visible rib definition, has barely palpable hip bones, and carries visible fat reserves at the tail base. The [crested gecko body condition score guide](/2026/08/17/crested-gecko-body-condition-score-underweight-healthy-and-overweight-examples/) provides photo references and palpation guidance for evaluating condition accurately - worth reviewing before you make any pairing decision.

### The Optional Cooling Period

Crested geckos in New Caledonia experience seasonal temperature variation, and simulating a mild winter cooling period tends to increase breeding season productivity. The standard approach is to drop ambient temperatures to 65-68°F for six to eight weeks in fall or early winter. This is optional if your home already experiences natural seasonal fluctuations, but keepers in climate-controlled homes with stable year-round temperatures frequently report more clutches per season after implementing deliberate cooling.

### Feeding During Conditioning

During the conditioning phase, increase feeding frequency. Offer a complete crested gecko diet (CGD) five to seven nights per week rather than the maintenance level of three nights, and add gut-loaded feeder insects twice weekly. Calcium with D3 supplementation on every insect feeding is non-negotiable. Females that enter a breeding season well-stocked on calcium tolerate the metabolic demands of egg production with substantially less risk.

Its Bluetooth app connectivity lets you monitor conditions conveniently, so you can confirm a genuinely stable cool period rather than simply a few cold nights followed by warm swings.

## Crested Gecko Breeding Behavior: Courtship and Pairing

Once both animals are at proper weight and condition, introduce the male into the female's enclosure for supervised pairings. Always bring the male to the female's space, not the reverse. Introducing a female into a male's territory increases her stress and can trigger defensive responses from the resident animal.

### What Normal Courtship Looks Like

Courtship follows a recognizable pattern. The male will begin chirping or producing a rapid clicking vocalization, then closely follow the female while licking her skin. He will attempt to bite onto her crest, neck, or dorsal surface to position himself for mating. This bite is firm but typically causes only minor superficial scale disturbance in healthy adults. A bite that breaks the skin deeply or causes bleeding is a sign the pairing is not going well and should be interrupted.

Copulation is brief, usually two to five minutes. You do not need to observe it directly to confirm mating occurred. A female that was with a compatible male for even a single 30-minute supervised session will frequently produce fertile eggs. Two to three pairings per week for three to four weeks improves fertilization rates and is a practical target for most keepers.

After each pairing session, return the male to his own enclosure. Leaving pairs housed together full-time results in continuous harassment of the female, feeding refusals, weight loss, and bite wound infections. Supervised pairings with immediate separation is the standard approach among experienced crested gecko breeders for precisely this reason.

### Monitoring the Female During Breeding Season

Weigh the female weekly once pairings begin. A well-managed female should not lose more than 5 percent of her body weight between consecutive weigh-ins. If she drops more than that between clutches, remove the male for four to six weeks, focus on heavy feeding, and wait for full weight recovery before resuming pairings. Ignoring a downward trend because "she seems fine" is how breeding seasons end in veterinary emergencies.

## The Laying Box and Egg Collection

A gravid female without a suitable oviposition site will retain eggs. Egg retention (dystocia) is a veterinary emergency with high mortality risk. Set up a laying box the moment pairings begin - not after you suspect gravidity - because a laying box that goes unused costs nothing, while a missing laying box at the wrong moment costs everything.

### Setting Up the Laying Box

A plastic shoebox with a cut entry hole works well. Fill it with coconut fiber (eco earth) or sphagnum moss moistened to clumping consistency but without free water dripping when squeezed. Depth matters: provide at least 4 inches of substrate so the female can dig a proper oviposition chamber. Place the box on the enclosure floor.

A gravid female typically shows visible eggs when her body wall is illuminated with a penlight, digs frequently, reduces food intake, and looks visibly distended in the lower abdomen. The interval from first mating to first clutch is usually 30 to 40 days. Subsequent clutches follow every four to six weeks throughout the active season.

### Egg Collection Procedure

Follow this sequence every single time eggs are found:

1. Check the laying box every two to three days once you suspect gravidity.
2. When eggs are found, note their exact position in the substrate before touching them.
3. Using a soft pencil, mark a small "X" on the top surface of each egg immediately. This is the orientation marker.
4. Carefully lift each egg and transfer it to your prepared incubation container with the marked side facing up.
5. Never rotate or invert eggs after marking. The embryo begins attaching to the upper surface of the egg within 12 to 24 hours of laying, and inverting a developing egg tears this attachment and kills the embryo.
6. Label the incubation container with the female's ID, clutch number, and collection date.

Crested geckos lay two eggs per clutch as the standard. Single-egg clutches and occasional three-egg clutches occur but are not common.

## Egg Incubation: Parameters, Setup, and Timeline

The safe incubation temperature range for crested gecko eggs is 68 to 78°F. Temperatures above 80°F cause embryonic death and developmental deformities at a high rate. Temperatures in the low 60s pause development without immediate harm but extend timelines significantly. The table below summarizes expected outcomes across the practical range:

| Incubation Temperature | Expected Hatch Time | Notes |
|---|---|---|
| 68-70°F | 90-120+ days | Safe range; longest timeline |
| 72-74°F | 60-90 days | Optimal for room-temperature incubation |
| 75-78°F | 50-75 days | Near upper safe limit; monitor closely |
| Above 80°F | Variable/failed | High deformity and embryo death risk |

### Step-by-Step Incubation Container Setup

1. **Choose your substrate.** Use medium-grade perlite or horticultural vermiculite. Perlite is more forgiving of minor over-hydration; vermiculite holds moisture slightly longer between checks.
2. **Mix substrate and water by weight, not volume.** Combine equal grams of dry substrate and water. The result should hold its shape when squeezed and feel damp throughout but release no free water.
3. **Fill the container.** Use a deli cup, small plastic food container, or a dedicated egg box with a lid. Fill to approximately 2 inches of depth.
4. **Create egg depressions.** Use your fingertip or a pencil eraser to form small indentations spaced at least 1 inch apart.
5. **Place eggs orientation-side up.** Set each egg into a depression so roughly half of the egg sits below the surface. Do not fully bury eggs.
6. **Close the lid loosely.** Leave it slightly ajar or punch two to four pinhole-sized vents. Fully sealed containers accumulate harmful CO2; fully open containers dry out too quickly.
7. **Label, date, and place.** Mark the container with clutch info and date, then place it in your incubation location. Verify temperature at the substrate level using a probe thermometer - air temperature and substrate temperature often diverge by 2 to 4 degrees inside enclosed containers.

For keepers running multiple clutches per season, or in homes where summer ambient temperatures exceed 78°F, a dedicated temperature-regulated incubator removes the uncertainty. The [Zoo Med Laboratories Reptibator Egg Incubator](https://www.amazon.com/dp/B00YYG3SQO?tag=scaledkeeper-20) is designed for reptile egg incubation, making it the practical choice over ambient room-temperature setups when consistency is the priority.

Candle eggs at 14 and 30 days to check viability. A developing egg shows a visible network of blood vessels and a dark embryo mass when held against a bright LED. An infertile egg (a "slug") looks uniformly yellow-white and opaque with no internal structure. Remove slugs promptly to prevent mold from spreading to healthy eggs nearby.

## Hatchling Setup and First 30 Days

Hatchlings emerge weighing 2 to 3 grams and measuring 3 to 4 inches in total length. House each hatchling individually from day one. Even same-clutch siblings will outcompete each other for food within weeks as growth rates diverge, and the larger animal will injure smaller ones. Tail loss in crested geckos is permanent - the tail does not regenerate - so any avoidable source of tail-loss risk is worth eliminating from the start.

### Enclosure Size and Environment

Start with a small arboreal enclosure, roughly 6 inches wide by 6 inches deep by 12 inches tall. This size provides sufficient vertical space for climbing behavior without forcing the hatchling to search an oversized space to locate its CGD dish. Oversized enclosures increase stress and contribute to feeding refusals in hatchlings that are already adjusting to a new environment.

Maintain temperatures of 72-78°F during the day, with nighttime drops into the upper 60s acceptable. Humidity should cycle between 60 and 80 percent. Mist once in the evening and allow the enclosure to partially dry before the following evening misting. Consistently high humidity without a drying period leads to respiratory issues and scale rot in hatchlings, which are more vulnerable than adults to stagnant wet conditions.

### First Feed and Growth Tracking

Hatchlings typically shed within 24 to 72 hours of hatching. Do not offer food until after that first shed is complete. Once the shed is finished, place a small amount of age-appropriate CGD on a shallow bottle cap positioned near a regular perch site. Most hatchlings begin eating within three to seven days of their first shed.

Introduce feeder insects at four to six weeks of age, once the hatchling is eating CGD reliably. Use the smallest available crickets or black soldier fly larvae, sized no wider than the space between the animal's eyes. Calcium with D3 supplementation on every insect feeding applies to hatchlings just as it does to adults.

Weigh hatchlings weekly and record the results. A healthy hatchling gains roughly 0.5 to 1 gram per week in the first month. A hatchling that is not gaining weight after two to three weeks on CGD warrants closer attention. Check husbandry conditions first - temperature, humidity, hide availability, and disturbance frequency. If nothing resolves the issue after two additional weeks of careful management, consult a reptile veterinarian.

## Common Crested Gecko Breeding Mistakes to Avoid

**Starting with an underweight female.** The 35-gram floor is a minimum floor, not a recommendation. Entering a breeding season at 36 grams means the female has essentially no metabolic buffer for the demands of egg production. By the third or fourth clutch, that deficit becomes visible as weight loss and behavioral change. The practical standard is 40 grams minimum, with 42 to 45 grams as an ideal starting weight for a first-season female.

**Skipping egg orientation marking.** This is the most common and most destructive egg-handling error. The embryo begins attaching to the upper surface of the egg within hours of laying. Moving an unmarked egg without knowing its original position risks inverting it. Keep a soft pencil within reach of every laying box, and mark each egg before doing anything else with it.

**Incubating too warm.** Sustained temperatures above 80°F reliably produce deformities and embryo death in crested gecko eggs. Keepers in warm climates who incubate at room temperature often lose entire summer clutches to ambient heat spikes without identifying the cause. If your home exceeds 78°F for extended stretches during warm months, a temperature-regulated incubator is not a luxury - it is a requirement.

**Leaving pairs together unsupervised.** Male crested geckos harass females continuously when housed together long-term. The female stops eating, loses weight, develops bite wounds that can become infected, and declines in body condition across weeks. Supervised pairings with immediate separation afterward is the baseline management practice, not an advanced technique reserved for difficult pairs.

**Ignoring female weight loss between clutches.** Weekly weighing during the breeding season exists to catch gradual decline before it becomes severe. A female losing meaningful weight between clutches is depleting reserves faster than she is rebuilding them. The fix is pulling the male, feeding heavily, and waiting for full recovery. Catching a 5-percent loss is manageable. Catching a 15-percent loss is a veterinary situation that could have been prevented with consistent monitoring.

**Not having a laying box ready.** Egg retention requires veterinary intervention and carries serious mortality risk. A laying box takes ten minutes to set up and costs almost nothing in materials. Set it up when pairings begin, not when you notice the female digging at the glass at 11 PM.

## Frequently Asked Questions

**How long does crested gecko egg incubation take?**

At standard room temperatures between 72 and 74°F, crested gecko eggs typically hatch in 60 to 90 days. Cooler incubation at 68 to 70°F extends the timeline to 90 to 120 days or more without harming healthy embryos, as long as temperatures stay above roughly 65°F consistently. Warmer incubation in the upper 70s can shorten timelines slightly but increases deformity risk as temperatures approach 80°F. If eggs have not hatched by 120 days at room temperature, candle them carefully before assuming failure. Late-season clutches incubated through seasonal temperature dips sometimes take 130 to 150 days to hatch successfully.

**Can crested gecko hatchlings be housed together?**

Housing hatchlings together is not recommended. Even clutch-mates that emerge at the same size diverge in growth rate within a few weeks, and the larger animal outcompetes the smaller for food. Injuries including tail loss are common in co-housed juveniles, and tail loss in crested geckos is permanent - the tail does not regenerate. Individual housing from hatch through the juvenile phase, roughly 10 to 15 grams, is the standard among experienced breeders and is the approach supported by the broader keeper community at resources like [Pangea Reptile](https://pangeareptile.com/). The convenience of shared housing is not worth the permanent cost to the smaller animal.

**How many clutches can a female crested gecko produce per year?**

A well-conditioned female on a quality diet typically produces four to eight clutches annually during the active breeding season, with two eggs per clutch as the standard. Clutch frequency depends on female condition, temperature, pairing schedule, and diet quality. Females maintained at 40-plus grams with regular insect supplementation and high-quality CGD reliably produce six to eight clutches without significant health impact. Breeding seasons longer than eight or nine months per year carry increasing risk for most females without a structured off-season recovery period. Weekly weight tracking is the most reliable way to identify when a female is producing faster than she is recovering.

**When should I contact a vet about a gravid female?**

Seek veterinary care promptly if a gravid female shows visible straining without egg deposition for more than 24 hours, lethargy combined with complete food refusal lasting more than five days, a prolapsed cloaca or visible tissue outside the vent, or a sudden sharp weight drop paired with behavioral changes. Egg retention can progress from manageable to life-threatening rapidly, and early intervention is far more effective than waiting. The [Association of Reptilian and Amphibian Veterinarians](https://www.arav.org/) maintains a searchable directory of qualified reptile vets if you do not already have one established before breeding season begins.

## Final Thoughts on Crested Gecko Breeding

Crested gecko breeding rewards the same systematic approach that good husbandry requires: consistent monitoring, honest assessment of your animals' condition, and preparation that happens before problems arise rather than in response to them. The parameters are genuinely forgiving compared to many reptile species, but that forgiveness disappears when females enter breeding season underweight, eggs are mishandled at collection, or hatchlings are set up in conditions that work against them.

Get your female to 40 grams before introducing a male. Mark every egg at the moment of collection. Keep incubation below 78°F. House hatchlings individually, weigh them weekly, and wait for the first shed before offering food. These five practices address the majority of crested gecko breeding failures before they happen. If your basic husbandry is solid and your animals are in good condition, a well-planned first breeding season is a straightforward and genuinely rewarding next step.
