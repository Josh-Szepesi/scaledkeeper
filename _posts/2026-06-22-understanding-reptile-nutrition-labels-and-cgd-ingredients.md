## Why Reptile Nutrition Labels Matter for Your Gecko

Unlike mammals, reptiles have fundamentally different nutritional requirements driven by their metabolism, activity level, and wild feeding ecology. A crested gecko in nature eats insects, fruit, and occasional flower nectar. Its digestive system evolved to extract maximum nutrition from food that varies seasonally. Commercial CGD (Crested Gecko Diet) attempts to replicate that complete nutrition in powder form, which is why every number on the label directly affects your gecko's health.

The stakes of nutritional imbalance are particularly high because symptoms often appear slowly and subtly. A crested gecko with inadequate calcium won't show obvious signs for weeks - but when metabolic bone disease appears, damage is already done. Poor protein or fat balance doesn't cause immediate illness either, but growth delays, lethargy, or reproductive failure can result months later. By then, the keeper often doesn't connect the problem to diet choices made half a year prior.

According to the [Association of Reptilian and Amphibian Veterinarians (ARAV)](https://www.arav.org), reptile nutrition labels differ significantly from mammal food labels. Pet food is heavily regulated and standardized, but reptile diets have far less regulatory oversight, meaning manufacturers aren't required to list nutrients the same way twice. One company might list "crude protein" while another lists "digestible protein"—the numbers look completely different even if usable nutrition is similar. You need to know what you're comparing.

Crested geckos specifically depend on commercial diets as their primary nutrition source in captivity. Unlike some reptiles that supplement with live insects and vegetables, most keepers feed exclusively CGD. This means the diet you choose becomes your gecko's entire nutritional foundation. Reading the label correctly isn't optional - it's the difference between a thriving animal and a declining one. Understanding what manufacturers are trying to communicate on their labels helps you recognize quality products and feeding protocols that will actually work.

## Understanding Protein and Fat Content in CGD

Protein and fat are the energy currencies of your gecko's diet. Crested geckos are omnivorous with a strong fruit preference, but they're not fruit-only animals - they eat insects in the wild, which means they need both plant and animal protein. The protein percentage on a CGD label tells you how much of the total diet (by weight) consists of amino acids and protein compounds. For crested geckos, appropriate protein content ranges from 8% to 15%, depending on age and life stage.

Why the variation? Younger, growing geckos and breeding females have higher protein demands than adults in maintenance mode. A juvenile might benefit from CGD closer to 12-15% protein to support growth, while an adult in maintenance thrives on 8-10% protein. Some manufacturers produce separate juvenile and adult formulations specifically to address this difference. The [Repashy Crested Gecko Diet Mango Superblend](https://www.amazon.com/dp/B0D84QQFP3?tag=scaledkeeper-20) sits at approximately 9% protein, positioning it as an adult maintenance diet, while other formulations run higher to support juveniles.

Fat isn't an enemy in reptile nutrition - it's fuel. Crested geckos need dietary fat for energy, vitamin absorption, and reproductive health. CGD fat percentages typically range from 4% to 8% for complete diets. Too much fat (above 8%) can lead to obesity in captive animals with limited activity. Too little (below 3%) leaves your gecko undernourished and sluggish. Manufactured formulations balance fat for palatability - fats make food taste better - with your gecko's actual nutritional needs.

When comparing brands, don't assume higher protein is automatically better. A diet at 15% protein might be designed as a breeding diet for females or a growth diet for juveniles, while one at 9% protein targets adult maintenance. Using the wrong product for your gecko's life stage can cause problems. An adult on juvenile growth diet might develop metabolic issues from excess nutrition; a growing juvenile on maintenance diet might not receive enough protein for proper development.

## Calcium, Phosphorus, and the Ca:P Ratio Explained

Calcium and phosphorus are the skeletal minerals that define reptile nutrition success. These two elements must exist in the right balance - the calcium-to-phosphorus ratio (Ca:P)—and for crested geckos, that ideal ratio is approximately 1.5:1 to 2:1. According to [veterinary reptile nutrition standards](https://www.vin.com), this narrow range is critical because phosphorus blocks calcium absorption when present in excess. Even if calcium is abundant in the diet, too much phosphorus prevents your gecko's digestive system from actually using it.

Here's why this matters: phosphorus is naturally abundant in most food sources, especially insects and grains. Calcium is naturally scarce. In the wild, crested geckos get calcium from insect exoskeletons and occasional snails. In captivity, manufacturers must actively add calcium to compensate. That's why "complete" CGD formulations are actually complete - they've added extra calcium to create the proper ratio relative to phosphorus naturally present in base ingredients.

When reading a CGD label, look for calcium and phosphorus percentages. A product listing "Calcium 2.0%" and "Phosphorus 1.0%" gives you a 2:1 ratio, which is ideal. Another at "Calcium 1.5%" and "Phosphorus 1.2%" provides 1.25:1, still acceptable but leaning lower. A third at "Calcium 1.2%" and "Phosphorus 1.1%" gives 1.09:1, below optimal and suggesting you'll need more supplementation than a properly formulated diet requires.

This is why supplementation is necessary even with commercial "complete" diets. Most manufacturers formulate products to be complete *with weekly supplementation*. They account for you adding extra calcium once per week via supplement. If you use a complete diet without supplementation, you're actually running a mineral deficit that compounds over months.

Vitamin D3 plays a crucial role here. D3 is necessary for calcium absorption and utilization. Some complete diets include D3; others don't. If your diet includes D3, your supplementation needs are lower. If it doesn't, supplementation becomes more critical. [Zoo Med ReptiCalcium Without Vitamin D3](https://www.amazon.com/dp/B002GCAUAA?tag=scaledkeeper-20) is specifically designed to be used alongside a diet that already contains D3, not as your sole calcium source.

## How to Read Crested Gecko Nutrition Labels and Ingredient Lists

Reading a CGD nutrition label becomes simple once you know what to look for. Follow these steps to evaluate any crested gecko diet:

1. Check the protein percentage. Look for total protein content between 8% and 15%. Verify this is appropriate for your gecko's life stage - juveniles and breeding females benefit from the higher range (12-15%), while adults in maintenance do well at 8-10%. This tells you whether the diet supports your gecko's specific growth and reproductive needs.

2. Verify the fat content. Confirm fat percentage falls between 4% and 8%. Fats provide essential energy and support vitamin absorption. Too little creates nutritional deficiency; too much promotes obesity in sedentary captive animals.

3. Calculate the calcium-to-phosphorus ratio. Find both calcium and phosphorus percentages on the label, then divide calcium by phosphorus. The result should be 1.5:1 to 2:1. For example, a diet with 2.0% calcium and 1.0% phosphorus gives a 2.0:1 ratio (ideal), while 1.5% calcium and 1.2% phosphorus gives 1.25:1 (acceptable but lower). This ratio determines how well your gecko can actually absorb and utilize available minerals.

4. Review the ingredient list. Scan the first five ingredients. Quality diets list fruit first (appropriate since wild geckos prefer fruit), followed by specific insect sources and added calcium. Avoid products where grains appear in the top five ingredients before calcium, or where ingredients are vaguely labeled. Look for transparency - "cricket meal" is better than "generic insect meal."

5. Check for vitamin D3 inclusion. Scan the ingredient list or contact the manufacturer to confirm whether the diet includes vitamin D3. If present, you'll supplement with calcium *without* D3. If absent, you need a supplement that includes D3. This prevents vitamin D3 toxicity from accumulation.

Once you've completed these five steps, you understand the nutritional foundation of the diet and can confidently choose supplementation and feeding frequency. Most complete diets will pass all five checks - your role is confirming they're right for your gecko and supplementing accordingly.

Ingredient lists are ordered by weight, with the first ingredients making up the bulk. On a quality crested gecko diet, fruit appears first - appropriate since wild crested geckos prefer fruit. What comes next reveals the nutritional architecture.

A quality CGD ingredient list might read: "Mango, insect meal (roaches, crickets, silkworm pupae), calcium carbonate, vitamin and mineral premix, fish oil, probiotics." This tells you the diet is mango-based, includes multiple insect types for protein diversity, has added calcium, includes targeted vitamin/mineral supplementation, contains omega fatty acids from fish oil, and includes probiotics for digestion.

A lower-quality list might read: "Fruit concentrate, grain filler, generic insect meal, calcium source, synthetic vitamin mix." The differences are subtle but significant. "Fruit concentrate" is vague - it could be anything. "Grain filler" suggests wheat, corn, or rice used primarily to bulk the product cheaply rather than add nutrition. "Generic insect meal" leaves you guessing about amino acid profiles.

Red flags: products listing fruit first but then showing several grain-based fillers in the top five ingredients (suggesting fruit flavor is marketing, not actual content). Products with "artificial flavoring" should make you question why flavor needs adding if fruit should provide palatability. Artificial preservatives (BHA, BHT, ethoxyquin) are concerning; look for natural preservatives like mixed tocopherols.

Watch for digestibility claims. "Crude protein" is total nitrogen converted to protein - it doesn't account for what your gecko actually absorbs. "Digestible protein" or "available protein" is lower but represents what your gecko truly uses. Some products list both; others only one. Premium manufacturers like Zoo Med and Repashy typically provide more transparency than budget brands, which is worth the modest cost difference.

For crested geckos specifically, identify whether the diet is fruit-forward or protein-forward. The [Repashy Crested Gecko Diet Mango Superblend](https://www.amazon.com/dp/B0D84QQFP3?tag=scaledkeeper-20) is notably mango-based and fruit-forward. Pangea Fruit Mix with Insects leans similar. Other brands may emphasize higher insect content. Neither is wrong - it depends on your gecko's natural preference. Some geckos strongly prefer fruit; others enjoy insect-protein bases. The ingredient list reveals which gecko personality each diet serves.

## Common Nutrition Mistakes and Supplementation Problems

The most pervasive mistake is misunderstanding "complete." Complete does not mean "never supplement this diet." Complete means "this diet contains all essential nutrients in proper balance *when used with occasional supplementation as directed*." Manufacturers assume you'll dust with calcium weekly or powder the food monthly. Using a complete diet without supplementation creates a deficit.

This mistake manifests as metabolic bone disease appearing months after starting a complete diet that should have prevented it. The keeper assumes the diet handles all nutrition, so they don't supplement. The diet assumes supplementation happens. The calcium deficit compounds over time until symptoms appear - lethargy, limb swelling, jaw softening. The diet wasn't the problem; the lack of supplementation was.

The opposite mistake is over-supplementation. Some keepers, worried about deficiency, add calcium powder multiple times weekly, add vitamins multiple times weekly, and treat supplementation as primary nutrition. This creates excess calcium and vitamin imbalances. Too much calcium blocks other mineral absorption (magnesium, zinc, iron). Excess vitamin A causes toxicity. Excess vitamin D3 causes hypercalcemia and kidney damage. More supplementation is worse.

The correct approach is simple: use a complete diet as primary nutrition, then add [Zoo Med ReptiCalcium Without Vitamin D3](https://www.amazon.com/dp/B002GCAUAA?tag=scaledkeeper-20) once weekly by light dusting or mixing into food. That's it. Don't add additional multivitamins unless a veterinarian identifies a specific deficiency. Trust the formulation.

Mixing multiple complete diets without calculating combined mineral load creates hidden imbalances. If you feed Repashy on Monday and Pangea on Wednesday, then add calcium supplement, you've potentially created an imbalance. You don't need the exact same product every meal, but avoid switching between multiple complete diets within the same week. Rotate products over weeks or months instead.

Inadequate feeding frequency is another hidden problem. If CGD is formulated for 2-3 times weekly feeding but you only feed once per week, your gecko isn't receiving calculated nutrition. The recommended amounts are tied to frequency. Feed once weekly instead of twice, and you've cut nutrition in half.

Improper storage accelerates nutrient degradation. Vitamins (especially A and D3) break down when exposed to light, heat, and humidity. Store mixed CGD in the refrigerator in airtight containers and use within 1-2 weeks. Store dry powder in a cool, dark, dry place within labeled shelf life. Expired or improperly stored CGD has lower nutrient bioavailability.

Inconsistent supplementation creates periods of calcium deficit that compound over months. Establish a routine. Mark your calendar. Set a phone reminder. Supplementation only works if it's consistent.

**Q: Do I need to supplement if I'm using a complete crested gecko diet?**

A: Yes, you need minimal supplementation even with complete diets. Manufacturers formulate complete diets assuming you'll add calcium supplementation approximately once per week. This supplementation creates the proper calcium-to-phosphorus ratio that the diet is engineered to work with. You don't need elaborate multi-step supplementation schedules - just grab [Zoo Med ReptiCalcium Without Vitamin D3](https://www.amazon.com/dp/B002GCAUAA?tag=scaledkeeper-20) and dust or powder your gecko's food lightly once weekly. Using a complete diet without supplementation means you're running a deficit relative to what the formulation was designed to provide. However, don't over-supplement. More than once weekly is unnecessary and creates vitamin and mineral imbalances that cause problems over months.

**Q: What's the difference between Repashy and Pangea crested gecko diets?**

The choice comes down to your gecko's feeding preference - some geckos strongly prefer one texture or flavor profile. Try both in small containers if possible and observe which your gecko eats more eagerly. Nutritionally, either will support a healthy gecko with proper supplementation.

**Q: What does the calcium-to-phosphorus ratio actually do in my gecko's body?**

A: Calcium and phosphorus build and maintain skeletal structure (bone and cartilage), regulate nerve and muscle function, and support reproductive health. However, phosphorus blocks calcium absorption when present in excess. If phosphorus is too high relative to calcium, your gecko's body can't absorb and utilize available calcium, leading to nutritional deficiency even when calcium is present in the diet. This is why ratio matters more than absolute calcium percentage. A diet with 2.0% calcium and 1.0% phosphorus (2:1 ratio) supports proper absorption. A diet with 2.0% calcium and 1.8% phosphorus (1.1:1 ratio) creates absorption problems because phosphorus interferes with calcium uptake. Over months on an imbalanced diet, this manifests as metabolic bone disease - soft bones, swollen jaws, lethargy, and skeletal deformity. Reading phosphorus content, not just calcium, is critical.

**Q: Should I use a calcium supplement with vitamin D3 or without?**

A: Most crested gecko CGD formulations already include vitamin D3. If your diet contains D3, you should use a supplement without D3—like [Zoo Med ReptiCalcium Without Vitamin D3](https://www.amazon.com/dp/B002GCAUAA?tag=scaledkeeper-20)—to avoid excess D3 accumulation over time. Excess vitamin D3 causes hypercalcemia and kidney damage. If your diet doesn't contain D3, you need a supplement that includes it. Check your diet's ingredient list or contact the manufacturer to verify D3 content. Most complete diets do include D3, so Zoo Med ReptiCalcium Without D3 is the safer choice for most keepers.
